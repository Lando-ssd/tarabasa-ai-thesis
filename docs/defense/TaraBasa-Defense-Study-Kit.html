How to read the words in square brackets

Every basis in this kit carries one of three labels. Learn them, because they are how you stay honest in front of a panel:

  • [CURRICULUM]: it comes from a DepEd document (MATATAG, Phil-IRI, DepEd Memorandum 173). Say "DepEd's" and name the document.
  • [RESEARCH]: it comes from a published study. The study was found by search and its title and authors were confirmed, but the full paper was not always read. The manuscript's reference pack says which ones are VERIFIED, LISTED or RECALLED. Say "research found that..." and never invent a number from it.
  • [TEAM CHOICE]: the team decided this number or rule. There is no source for it. Say so plainly: "this is our own setting, kept in one settings file so it can be changed after real children use it." A panel respects this far more than a made-up citation.

Three rules for the whole defense

  1. Never say the AI was trained. No model was trained, because there is no collection of children's recordings. The system is a speech recognition service (Vosk), an AI language model that writes activity drafts (Gemini), and written rules and formulas. Say it before they ask.
  2. Never say it was tested with real children. It was tested with automated tests (775 passing), with computer voices, and in live use by the team. Real-child testing is the next step and the first limitation. Fill in the results of your teacher survey and user acceptance test only with the real numbers from your appendices.
  3. Never say DepEd approved it. It follows DepEd documents. It is not made or endorsed by the Department of Education (the home page says so).

The 12 sentences to know by heart

  1. TaraBasa AI is a reading practice and monitoring system for Grade 1 to 3 learners, with a phone app and a website for learners, teachers, parents and an administrator.
  2. A child reads aloud; a speech recognition service writes down the words; our rules compare them with the activity and decide what the child needs next.
  3. Teachers see results in Phil-IRI words (Independent, Instructional, Frustration). Children never see those words, only four friendly steps.
  4. A child's reading step does not depend on grade: a Grade 3 child who cannot read yet starts on letters.
  5. The first reading check has at most 3 texts: 90 percent or more moves up, under 70 moves down, 70 to 89 stops; letters move up at 80.
  6. After that, a child moves one step up after 3 strong readings (90 percent or more) from at least 2 activities, and one step down after 2 weak readings (under 70) in a row.
  7. Activities are written by an AI language model, but a teacher must approve each one and decides its level.
  8. Easy, Medium and Hard are this app's levels (word familiarity and length), not DepEd scores, and the teacher decides.
  9. Alerts always show their evidence and never act by themselves.
  10. A class holds one grade only; the school decides which grade a teacher teaches.
  11. A recording is deleted as soon as it is scored; there are no ads and no outside tracking.
  12. Everything adaptive is rule based and explainable; the numbers are the team's settings and are the first thing we will check with real children.

When you do not know the answer

Use this shape, it works for any question: (1) what we did, (2) what the data or the source shows, (3) what we did not measure, (4) what we would do next.

Part A. The hook (45 seconds)

Say it slowly. Pause after the question.

Read aloud

"Good morning, panel. Imagine a Grade 1 teacher with a class of more than thirty children and thirty minutes for reading. To know how each child reads, she must listen to every child, one by one, and write down what she hears. That is less than a minute per child, and the next lesson is waiting.

This is the real picture behind the numbers. In the 2022 PISA, the Philippines scored 347 in reading, about 76th out of 81 countries. The World Bank estimated that about nine in ten Filipino ten-year-olds cannot read and understand a simple story.

So we asked one question: what if every child had a patient listener, and every teacher got the results before the next lesson? That is TaraBasa AI."

Part B. The problem and the idea (1 minute 30 seconds)

Read aloud

"There are three problems behind the statistics.

First, listening to children read is the most reliable way to know how they read. DepEd's own tool, the Phil-IRI, is an oral reading test done one child at a time. It works, but it takes a teacher's time, so it is done a few times a year, not every week.

Second, children in Grades 1 to 3 are at very different points. In one classroom some children cannot name letters while others read short stories, and one set of worksheets cannot fit them all.

Third, parents want to help but do not know how, and teachers do not hear about a struggling child until it is late.

TaraBasa AI answers these with three promises. One: we listen. The child reads aloud into the phone and a speech recognition service writes down what was said. Two: we place and guide. Written rules, based on DepEd references, decide where the child is and what to read next. Three: we tell. Teachers and parents get clear results and alerts, with the evidence, so a person decides. Everything adaptive is explainable: it is a set of written rules, not a black box."

Notes for you, not spoken

Notes for you:

  • "Problem 1" is the Phil-IRI. See file 3, section 3, for what the Phil-IRI is. [CURRICULUM]
  • "Problem 3": the manuscript cites Hernandez and Decena (2024) on parent involvement and reading achievement (VERIFIED in the reference pack) and Kaliisa et al. (2024) on dashboards (VERIFIED). Use those two if asked for a source.
  • Say "explainable" on purpose. It is your main difference from a black box (file 3, section 24).

4The first reading check: where does this child start?

  • On the screen
    Tara says "Let's read together." A one-word warm-up checks the microphone ("say sun"). Then the child reads the first text. If it is a Grade 3 child who cannot read yet, the first text is six letters. After the text a screen says "Great job" and the next one begins. The check stops after at most three texts, then shows the result as a friendly step, never a score.
  • Behind the screen
    The recording goes to our server, then to the speech recognition service (Vosk), which writes down the words. Our rules line the heard words up against the text, count right, skipped and different words, and decide: 90 percent or more moves up one step, under 70 percent moves down one step, 70 to 89 percent stops. Letters move up at 80 percent. The step is saved.
  • Why it exists
    A child's grade is not a child's reading level. The check finds where each child really is. The texts are curated and coded to the MATATAG curriculum, so they are always the same quality, they open instantly, and they cost no AI call. [CURRICULUM for the codes; TEAM CHOICE for 90, 70, 80]

    The child sees one of four steps: Letter Explorer, Word Builder, Sentence Reader or Story Reader. The parent sees the same four steps, with a plain sentence. Only the teacher sees the Phil-IRI words.

"We never show a child a score on the first check. We show a friendly step. The check is not a test to pass; it is a place to start."

  • On the screen

Tapping the flame or the star opens a card that explains the number.
  • Behind the screen
    The path is read from the child's saved step. "Great readings so far" counts readings of 90 percent or more since the last move.
  • Why it exists
    Children aged six to nine understand "where am I on the road" far better than a number. The path always moves forward in small steps so they can see progress. The four steps follow how the curriculum teaches reading: letters, then words, then sentences, then stories. [CURRICULUM]
  • Where it ends
    The child taps Start Reading.

  • On the screen
    The Read tab shows what the teacher gave the child and what a parent unlocked. The recommended one is first, marked "Picked just for you". A comprehension story ends with two or three questions.
  • Behind the screen
    On the website the recording is saved for a few seconds as a temporary file and a background worker scores it, so the web server is never held up; the page asks "is it done?" every two seconds. The worker calls the speech service, cleans the result (words that sound the same are accepted, words heard only faintly are not counted against the child), saves the reading, updates the word bank, points, streak and badges, checks whether the child should move a step, tells the teacher and parents, and asks the recommender what comes next. Then the recording is deleted.
  • Why it exists
    The free tries let a child practise without pressure. The results screen is always a happy star, because a low score on a first try is not a reason to feel bad. The recording is deleted because we do not need it.

    The child moves on, or tries a game.

"The star is always happy. We never tell a six-year-old they failed. We tell the teacher, who can do something about it."

    A teacher should look at the children who need help today, and see why, not a wall of numbers. The suggestion saves planning time but the teacher stays in charge.

3The main flows, step by step

Flow A. Sign up, confirm the email, sign in (parent or teacher)

  1. The user fills the form on the website, or in the app, which sends the same request to the JSON interface (/api/auth/register/...). Both go through one shared service on the server, so the rules cannot differ between web and app.
  2. The server validates: names are letters only, the password is at least 8 characters, the two passwords match, the email's domain can receive mail (a typo like gmial.com gets a "Did you mean gmail.com?"), the consent box is ticked.
  3. It writes the account to the database: the password is hashed (a one-way scramble; nobody, including us, can read it), a parent or teacher profile row, and a consent record (which policy version was shown, the time, web or app). A teacher starts as Pending.
  4. It sends the confirmation email through the Gmail API (HTTPS with an OAuth token, from the project's own Gmail account). The link is signed (it cannot be forged) and expires in 60 minutes. If the email cannot be sent, the account is removed again so nothing unusable is left.
  5. The user clicks the link; the server checks the signature, marks the email as confirmed, and shows "Email confirmed".
  6. Sign in: on the website the server creates a session, stored in the database, and gives the browser a cookie. In the app it issues a Sanctum token that the app keeps on the phone and sends with every request. A wrong password five times locks sign-in for a minute. A teacher who is not approved yet can sign in but cannot touch any real child.

Why the email step exists: it proves the address is real, and it protects against someone creating an account for another person.

Flow B. A child logs in

  1. The child scans the QR card (the camera is read on the phone; only the code leaves) or types the code, then the PIN.
  2. The server checks the code's check digit first (so a typo is caught without a database search), then finds the child and compares the hashed PIN.
  3. Wrong PIN five times: locked for 15 minutes, and every linked guardian gets an alert with what to do. The error for a wrong code and a wrong PIN looks the same, so nobody can find out which codes exist.
  4. A child's session is separate from an adult's, so a parent and a child can be signed in on the same device.

Flow C. The first reading check

  1. On first login the server notes the child has no check yet and sends the child to the check.
  2. It chooses the first text from a curated bank (21 texts, coded to MATATAG, stored in the app) by the parent's answers, not by grade. This is instant; no AI is called.
  3. The child records. The app or page records audio, the microphone is first tested on the device (silence is caught before anything is sent).
  4. The recording is uploaded to the server over HTTPS.
  5. The server sends it to the reading service. The service converts it to 16 kHz audio (ffmpeg), runs Vosk speech recognition, and returns the heard words with timing and confidence, and a few scores (words per minute, a speed score, a prosody score).
  6. Our server cleans and compares: it lines the heard words up against the text, accepts words that sound the same, leaves faintly heard words out ("Not sure"), and for letters repairs known recognizer habits. It computes the accuracy.
  7. It applies the staircase (90 up, under 70 down, 70 to 89 stop; letters 80 up), picks the next text or finishes, saves the child's step and the first badge, and tells the parent.
  8. The recording is deleted.

Flow D. A normal reading (what the child does every day)

  1. The child opens a story (assigned by a teacher, unlocked by a parent, or recommended) and records.
  2. Website: the page uploads the recording in the background and gets a ticket number. A queue worker takes the job, calls the reading service, cleans the result, and saves it; the page asks "done yet?" every 2 seconds and shows the result. This design keeps the web server free; before it, one slow recording could block every other user. Phone app: the scoring runs inside the upload request itself, with at most two at a time. If the server is busy the app says so and offers Send Again. (This difference is known and listed in the limits.)
  3. After scoring, the server saves the reading, works out points (about half the accuracy percent), the streak, the weekly goal and the badges, adds missed words to the word bank, checks the progression rule (3 strong readings to move up, 2 weak to move down), creates the teacher's and parents' notifications, and calls the recommender.
  4. The recommender receives the child's scores and returns an updated skill score and the next skill to practise. It keeps nothing; our database stores its answer.
  5. The recording file is deleted when the job ends.

Flow E. The teacher writes activities

  1. The teacher opens Generate, picks grade, skill, type, how many of each level, optional topic and class. If a class is chosen, the server builds a short note from the class's real readings (step, mistakes, missed words, liked topics) and shows it first.
  2. Pressing Write saves a request and returns at once. A background worker calls the activity writer, which calls Gemini. A request takes about two minutes. One free credit is used only when the activities are saved; a failure costs nothing. A new teacher has 2 credits; sharing an approved activity earns 2.
  3. The drafts appear. The teacher places each one in Easy, Medium or Hard, edits or rejects. Only an approved activity can be assigned.
  4. Assigning checks two rules on the server: the grade rule (an activity is only for classes and learners of its grade) and "can this child read it" (word count against the child's step).

Flow F. Alerts and analytics

  1. There is no separate alert job. When the teacher opens Alerts, Analytics or Home, the server reads the readings from the database and applies the written rules (latest reading under 70, last 3 at 90 or more, no reading for 7 days, and so on) and shows the evidence.
  2. A suggested activity is picked from the teacher's own approved activities, only if no child in the group would find it too long.

Flow G. Open Repository (the shared library)

  1. A teacher shares an approved activity, free or at a price (one time; the teacher earns 2 more credits).
  2. A parent browses it, with the best match for their child first, unlocks it for a child, and may rate it. The paid unlock is simulated: the screen says so and no real payment gateway exists.

Flow H. Deleting data

  1. A parent can delete a child. If another guardian is linked, it only removes this parent's link; otherwise it deletes everything about the child: readings, word bank, badges, alerts, consent records, photo and phone sign-ins.
  2. A teacher or parent can delete their own account with their password. The Admin can do it for someone who cannot sign in.
  3. A small log records that a deletion happened, with no name, email or code.

  • Why Grades 1 to 3. The first three years are when decoding is learned. A child who cannot decode by Grade 3 struggles in every subject after. A system that catches the problem early has the most value. It is also the age group the Phil-IRI and MATATAG treat as the foundation stage.

  • What it is. The child reads aloud and software writes down what it heard.
  • Why it exists
    Reading aloud shows accuracy and fluency directly, and it is how DepEd's own reading test works (the Phil-IRI is an oral reading test). A teacher cannot listen to thirty children every week; software can.
  • The basis. The Phil-IRI measures oral reading accuracy. [CURRICULUM] The National Reading Panel (2000) reported that guided repeated oral reading improves word recognition, fluency and comprehension; Therrien (2004) found the same for repeated reading in a meta-analysis.   • Why Vosk. It is open source (built on Kaldi; Povey et al., 2011, RECALLED), free per request, and we run it ourselves, so a child's voice is not sent to a large company. It returns each word with its time and a confidence. [TEAM CHOICE]

3Phil-IRI and the level names (Independent, Instructional, Frustration)

What is the Phil-IRI?

The Philippine Informal Reading Inventory is DepEd's tool for finding out how a learner reads. A teacher gives the learner graded passages, listens to the learner read aloud, counts the word reading errors, then asks comprehension questions. The results place the learner at a reading level. DepEd uses it as a pretest and posttest of the school year. The current policy is DepEd Order No. 14, s. 2018. [CURRICULUM, order number from search; DepEd's site did not open for us, so copy the exact title from deped.gov.ph]

What do the three levels mean?

A Non-reader is a learner who cannot yet recognize and sound out letters and simple words.

Where does the "Frustration level" idea come from?

It comes from the Informal Reading Inventory tradition in reading education: a teacher finds the level where a child reads easily alone (independent), learns best with help (instructional), and gives up or guesses (frustration). The Philippine version was adapted from that tradition; an older Phil-IRI document says its criteria were adapted from Johnson, Kress and Pikulski (1987). [RESEARCH: found by search, not read; I could not confirm the origin anywhere else, so say "adapted from the informal reading inventory tradition" and name Johnson, Kress and Pikulski only if you open the document] Caution on numbers: an older Phil-IRI document gives slightly different comprehension cut-offs (instructional 59 to 79, frustration 58 and below) from the 2018-era dashboards (60 and below 60). Our app uses only the word reading bands (97 and 90). It does not map its quiz to the comprehension bands.

What does TaraBasa do with the three levels?

  • For one reading, the name is given by accuracy: 97 or more Independent, 90 to 96 Instructional, under 90 Frustration. [CURRICULUM for the numbers]
  • For a child's standing, the child's reading step is mapped to a band: Letters is Non-reader, Words and Short sentences are Frustration, Sentences and Short stories are Instructional, Stories and Longer stories are Independent. That mapping is the team's own choice, not DepEd's. [TEAM CHOICE]
  • Who sees which words. Teachers see the Phil-IRI words (Non-reader, Frustration, Instructional, Independent). Children and parents both see the four friendly steps (next section): Letter Explorer, Word Builder, Sentence Reader, Story Reader. A parent also gets a plain sentence ("Mia is learning to sound out and read words") and a home tip. No one outside the teacher's screens sees the Phil-IRI words, and the system's own stored level (Beginning, Developing, Proficient) is shown to nobody. Why: "frustration level" sounds harsh to a parent, and a parent who sees the same step names as their child can talk about it together.
  • One more honest point. One source says the 2018 manual is written for Grades 3 to 6. Using the bands for a single passage read by a Grade 1 or 2 child is our own extension. And our moving-up rule (section 7) is a separate rule from the Phil-IRI bands; we show both and never claim they are the same thing.
  • How to say it. "We use DepEd's Phil-IRI words because teachers already know them. The three bands are about oral reading accuracy: 97 and above independent, 90 to 96 instructional, below 90 frustration. We borrowed the words; our rule for moving a child up or down is our own, and we say so."

5The reading path (the progress path) and why it fits Grades 1 to 3

  • What it is. A path on Home with the four steps, the child's step marked, and a ring that shows "great readings so far" toward the next step ("2 of 3").
  • Why it exists
    A young child needs to see progress and know what is next. It must move in small steps so it is visible. It is shown as a road, not a table, because "where am I on the road" is concrete.
  • Why it is connected to the child's reading step and not to the grade. In Grades 1 to 3 many children cannot read well yet, so their grade says little about what they can read. A Grade 3 child who cannot read yet starts on Letter Explorer; a Grade 2 child who reads well starts on stories. The grade still decides the class, the activities' grade and the curriculum code, but the step decides what the child is given to read. This follows the adviser's idea that the system should "find where the child is, not where the grade says". The first check's own documents say the same (a struggling Grade 3 reader may need Grade 1 content). [TEAM CHOICE, supported by MATATAG's grade by grade design and by the Phil-IRI idea of reading levels]
  • What makes it open and move. The path moves one step up after 3 strong readings and one step back after 2 weak readings (section 7). It never skips steps, and a single reading never moves a child.
  • How to say it. "The path is tied to the child's reading, not to the grade, because in Grades 1 to 3 the grade does not tell you who can read. The grade picks the class and the curriculum codes; the reading step picks the book."

6The first reading check

  • What it is. The first thing a new child does: at most 3 short texts, each read aloud.
  • Why it exists
    To place the child before anything else, so the first activities fit. Without it, every child would get Grade-level text.
  • The rule. [TEAM CHOICE]
    • It begins on the lowest step that the parent's description and the parent's three yes or no answers suggest. (The three questions change with the grade: Grade 1 asks about letters, sounds, blending; Grade 2 about short sentences, sight words, answering a question; Grade 3 about a paragraph, main idea, a word from context.)
    • 90 percent or more moves up one step; under 70 moves down one step; 70 to 89 stops. Letters move up at 80 because speech recognition is weakest on single letter names, and with six letters one miss is 83 percent.
    • The child is never shown a score. The dots show only how many texts have been read so far, never a total, because the check can stop after any text.
  • Why curated texts and not AI. The 21 check texts (3 for each of 7 steps) are written by the team and coded to MATATAG (for example EN2PWS-I-2 "identify alphabet letter names"). They open instantly (about 0.2 seconds, against 8 to 105 seconds when an AI wrote them), have the same difficulty for every child, and cost nothing per use. [CURRICULUM for the codes]
  • Honest limits. The texts were written by the team, so a Grade 1 and a Grade 2 teacher should review them. They were tested with computer voices only. Letter names are the hardest thing for speech recognition; we measured that and built a repair for known problems (section 8).
  • How to say it. "The first check is a placement, not a test. At most three texts. We start from the parent's answers so a child who cannot read does not face a story, and we never show the child a score."

7Moving a child up or down

  • What it is. After each real reading, the system decides whether the child stays, moves up one step or moves down one step.
  • The rule (settings file config/progression.php). [TEAM CHOICE]
    • Up: at least 3 readings of 90 percent or more, from at least 2 different activities, among the last 5 readings since the last move. The child moves one step.
    • A reading only counts if it has enough words for the step the child is leaving: Letters 4, Words 6, Short sentences 10, Sentences 17, Short stories 27, Stories 45.
    • Down: 2 readings in a row under 70 percent on texts that were not too long for the child. One weak reading never moves anyone.
  • Why this way. A single perfect reading of six words shows the child read those six words, not that they mastered a step. Reading programmes decide on repeated evidence; the Phil-IRI measures a whole passage, and repeated reading is what builds fluency (National Reading Panel, 2000; Therrien, 2004). Before this rule, one 90 percent reading could jump a child a whole level, which was a bug we found and fixed.
  • How to say it. "We do not move a child on one good day. Three strong readings from two different activities move a child up one step; two weak readings in a row move a child back one. The numbers are ours, in one settings file, and the first thing a real-children pilot should tune."

8How a reading is scored

  • What it is. Which words were read right, skipped, or said as a different word.
  • The steps. The recording goes to the speech service. The words it heard are lined up against the activity's words. Each word is right, skipped or a different word; extra words are noted. [TEAM CHOICE for the rules, applied to the service's output]
  • Fairness rules (these exist because the recognizer was built for adults):
    1. Same-sounding words are accepted (sun and son), spelling variants (mangoes and mangos), numbers (2 and two), a missing apostrophe, one word written as two (sand castle).
    2. Letter names repaired. When a child names letters, the recognizer may write "are" for R or "hr" for H then R. We repair only what the service marked wrong, only add credit, never move words, and a row said in the wrong order gets no extra credit. We measured this on 70 recordings with two computer voices: rows right went from 14 of 18 to 18 of 18 and no score went down.
    3. "Not sure" words. A word the recognizer heard as a different word but only faintly (confidence under 0.5) is left out of the score, the word bank and the alerts. At most 25 percent of the words can be "not sure", so it can never hide a reading that is mostly wrong.
    4. Near misses stay mistakes (ripe and right, hit and hat).
  • The honest limits. Names and rare same-sound words can still be marked wrong; the numbers were set with computer voices and need checking with children's recordings; we could not use the service's own pronunciation scores as a mark.
  • How to say it. "We do not trust the speech recognizer blindly. We accept same-sounding words, we leave out words it only heard faintly, and we repair the letter-name habits we measured. We never hide a mostly wrong reading, and the teacher sees the evidence."

9How the AI writes activities

  • What it is. A teacher asks for reading activities; an AI language model drafts them.
  • The path. Teacher picks grade, skill (foundational reading, reading fluency or reading comprehension), type of reading (word reading, sight words, phonics reading, sentence reading, passage reading, timed reading, repeated reading, reading comprehension), how many of each level, an optional topic and an optional class. Our server sends only a grade, a skill, a topic and a short note to the activity writer, which asks Gemini to write texts aligned to the MATATAG curriculum for that grade and returns all three levels. We keep the levels the teacher asked for.
  • Why an AI at all. A teacher cannot write hundreds of correctly leveled texts. A language model can draft them in two minutes. [RESEARCH: Gemini Team, 2023, LISTED]
  • Why a teacher must approve. A language model can write text that is wrong, too hard or off-topic. So every draft is checked by a teacher and placed in a level, or rejected. Only approved activities can be given to a child. This is called "human in the loop".
  • What the AI knows about the children. Nothing. When the teacher chooses "For a class", our server builds a short note from the class's own readings (the step most children are on, the kind of mistake several children make, the words several missed, the topics parents said children like) and the teacher reads it before pressing Write. A fact is only included when at least 2 children show it, so one child's habit is never presented as the class's. No name, code or single child's details are ever sent. The facts stay on the activity ("Written for Kammunggay because..."), so the reason can always be seen.
  • The "Did it work?" check. Analytics compares the same children's readings of activities written for their class against the teacher's general activities. A number is shown only when at least 3 children read both kinds, and a difference under 3 points is "about the same". It is a signal to watch, not proof, because a text can be easier or more interesting.
  • Credits. A new teacher has 2 free writing requests; sharing an approved activity earns 2 more. This limits the cost of AI use. [TEAM CHOICE]
  • How to say it. "The AI is a drafting assistant. It knows only a grade, a skill, a topic and a class summary, never a child. A teacher approves every draft and decides its level. And we show the teacher exactly what the AI was told."

10Easy, Medium and Hard: where the words and numbers come from

  • What it is. Three levels for each activity, shown to teachers with a one-line description.
  • The exact wording (it is on the Generate screen and in each activity's "Why this level" box):
    • Easy: "Familiar words, short text." Very familiar words that are easy to sound out, in the shortest text for the grade.
    • Medium: "Grade level words, longer text." Words a child at this grade should know, in text that is a little longer.
    • Hard: "Harder words, longest text." The hardest words a child at this grade should meet, in the longest text for the grade.
    • The box adds: "Levels come from how familiar the words are and how long the text is. They are this app's levels, not official DepEd scores. The AI cannot know what is hard for your learners, so you decide."
  • Where the numbers come from. The activity writer fixes how many words each level has, per type of reading and grade. Examples (spoken words a child reads): a word list for Grade 1 is Easy 5 to 7, Medium 8 to 10, Hard 11 to 14; for Grade 3 it is 8 to 10, 11 to 14, 15 to 20. A passage for Grade 1 is Easy 20 to 30, Medium 31 to 45, Hard 46 to 65; for Grade 3 it is 40 to 60, 61 to 90, 91 to 130. A comprehension text for Grade 2 is 35 to 50, 51 to 75, 76 to 105. We copy those bands so the teacher sees the range without waiting for the service. [TEAM CHOICE, the teammate's service]
  • Why they are not DepEd levels. The curriculum guides give no word counts, and there is no official "Easy, Medium, Hard" score. So we say plainly that these are the app's levels. That honesty is the basis.
  • Why three levels. Three is simple for a teacher to sort and matches the recommender's three difficulty bands (easy up to 59, medium up to 79, hard above). The difficulty a teacher chooses is what the recommender and the child's picker use.
  • How to say it. "Easy, Medium and Hard are our app's levels, based on how familiar the words are and how long the text is. They are not DepEd scores, because DepEd gives no such scale. That is why a teacher places every activity in its level."

  • What it is. A class holds one grade only, even if a teacher handles several grades.
  • The basis. The MATATAG curriculum is written grade by grade (DepEd Order No. 10, s. 2024). In a DepEd school the school head assigns which grade a teacher teaches, so a teacher cannot choose grades on their own: taking a grade away saves at once; adding a grade is a request that the Admin confirms with the school. [CURRICULUM; TEAM CHOICE for the check]
  • Multigrade. DepEd defines a multigrade class as two or more grades under one teacher (DepEd Orders 96 s. 1997 and 81 s. 2009, found by search; confirm on deped.gov.ph). We chose not to model it. A teacher with several grades opens one class for each. A Grade 2 learner cannot be added to a Grade 1 class, and a Grade 2 activity cannot be given to a Grade 1 class.
  • How the Add learner screen helps. As the teacher types a code, the system checks it. If the child is of another grade, the Add button stays off and says why.
  • How to say it. "A class is one grade because the curriculum is by grade and the school decides who teaches which grade. If a panelist asks about multigrade, say it is a deliberate scope choice: a multigrade teacher opens a class for each grade."

13Reading groups inside a class

  • What it is. Each class is split automatically into three groups: Needs most support (Non-reader and Frustration), Instructional, Independent. A teacher can assign an activity to one group.
  • Why it exists
    In one classroom children read at very different levels. Teaching in small groups at the child's instructional level is guided reading, which the MATATAG Key Stage 1 guidance describes. [CURRICULUM, from the guide; wording to confirm]
  • A child who improves stays in the same class (a class is a grade) and appears in the next group. Group assignments follow the child.
  • How to say it. "Teachers do not have to sort children by hand. The class is grouped by reading level, and the groups move as the children do."

14Alerts: when a teacher is told

  • The rules [TEAM CHOICE, numbers in how-it-works]:
    • Needs support: the latest reading is under 70 percent and less than 14 days old (stronger evidence if the last 3 were).
    • Ready to move up: the last 3 readings were 90 percent or more, not counting readings already used to move the child.
    • Moved up a level: the Phil-IRI name changed upward in the last 14 days.
    • Gone quiet: the child has read before but not for 7 days or more.
    • Just starting to read: the measured step is Letters. This is shown kindly, as where reading instruction begins.
  • Why 70 here and Phil-IRI's 90? The Phil-IRI judges a whole passage with a trained teacher. Our readings are short, so under 90 would fill the teacher's list with children at 85 to 89. Under 70 means "look at this child today". [TEAM CHOICE]
  • What an alert never does. It shows its evidence and one suggested activity from the teacher's own approved activities. It never gives anything to a child by itself. The teacher presses a button or marks it handled.
  • Why it exists
    Teachers should look at the children who need help today, and see why. Dashboards help most when they lead to an action (Kaliisa et al., 2024, VERIFIED: dashboards had a small effect on achievement but helped participation).
  • How to say it. "An alert is a reason to look, not a decision. It always shows the evidence."

15The "AI suggestion"

  • What it is. The suggested activity under an alert, and the "best match" order in the parent's library and the child's list.
  • How it picks (this is the honest part). It is rule-based scoring, not a learned model. For a child or group it takes the teacher's own approved activities, keeps only those of the right grade, drops any that is too long for anyone in the group (section 11), prefers the length closest to comfortable, prefers topics the parents said the children like, and prefers texts that practise the child's main mistake pattern (section 16).
  • For a child's own list the recommender's pick comes first ("Picked just for you"), then a story that practises the pattern ("Practice word endings"). Nothing is ever hidden. A text far too long goes last, marked "A bigger one for later".
  • How to say it. "Our suggestions are rules with reasons, so a teacher can ask why and get an answer."

17The recommender: "what to read next"

  • What it is. A small service, built by the team, that keeps a score for each skill and names the next skill to practise. It uses formulas, not machine learning.
  • The formulas (service version 1.0.0; confirm on its settings page). [TEAM CHOICE]
    • Attempt score: for foundational reading 0.80 accuracy plus 0.20 speed; for reading fluency 0.40 accuracy plus 0.35 speed plus 0.25 prosody; for reading comprehension the quiz score alone.
    • Skill score updates by a moving average: new score = 0.70 old plus 0.30 the new attempt (alpha 0.30), so one reading cannot swing it.
    • Difficulty from the score: up to 59 easy, up to 79 medium, above hard. Confidence starts at 0.60 and rises 0.08 per attempt up to 0.95. A level change needs 2 pieces of evidence, and the same skill is not chosen more than 2 times in a row.
  • A worked example for the panel. A reading has accuracy 87.5, speed 100, prosody 55. Fluency attempt = 87.5 x 0.40 + 100 x 0.35 + 55 x 0.25 = 35 + 35 + 13.75 = 83.75. If the old score was 97.4, the new score = 97.4 x 0.70 + 83.75 x 0.30 = 93.3. We confirmed the live service returned exactly these numbers.
  • The honest limits. The three skills are the service's own grouping; we translate them into the curriculum's subdomains (Phonics and Word Study, Comprehending and Analyzing Text). "Speed" is based on correct words per minute and is not adjusted by grade. The weights are the teammate's choice and are not from a study.
  • How to say it. "The recommender is a formula. It moves a skill score slowly so one reading cannot swing it, and it explains itself."

18The word bank

  • What it is. The words a child keeps missing.
  • The rules. After each scored reading, every missed word goes in once as Struggling. A word already there that the child reads right moves to Improving after the first correct reading and to Mastered after 3 correct readings. One reading adds at most 4 new words. A word the checker was only "not sure" about is never added.
  • Why. A word is practised soon after a miss and then less and less: spaced review. The cap stops one hard passage from flooding the bank.
  • The basis. [TEAM CHOICE] The idea of spaced review is common in learning research, but no study was checked for these exact numbers. Say so.
  • Where it is used. Games offer these words first; Alerts and the parent's "Practise at home" list use them.

19The practice games

  • What they are. Balloon Pop (read a word), Word Train (read a row; a child on the Letters step gets rows of letters), Sentence Stars (read a sentence), Syllable Steps (split, join and read a longer word).
  • Why they exist. Practice should not feel like a test, and a child needs more reading than a teacher can assign. Every game ends with the child reading out loud, because tapping alone is not learning. [RESEARCH: Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015, LISTED: children learn best from apps that are active, engaged, meaningful and social]
  • The rules [TEAM CHOICE, in config/practice_games.php]. A round has 3 things to read; each gets up to 2 tries; "Tara is not sure" is shown, never "wrong"; 2 or more right the first time climbs a level; a sitting is up to 3 rounds. A row or sentence counts as read when at least 66 percent of its words are heard. The level a game starts at follows the child's step. A game is free play: it never changes points, streak or level, and nothing the child says is kept.
  • Why free play. If games changed the real level, a child could repeat an easy game to climb. The real level changes only from real readings.
  • What each game offers first. The child's own missed words, then words that practise the child's main mistake, then the level's list. The Games page marks one game as recommended.
  • How to say it. "Games are practice, not marks. Every one ends with the child reading aloud, and none can change the child's level."

20The syllable method (pamamaraang pantig)

  • What it is. Splitting a longer word into syllables and joining it again: hear and echo, split it, join it, read it in a sentence. Only the last step is scored.
  • The basis. The MATATAG Grade 1 Reading and Literacy guide has the competencies RL1PA-I-2 ("segment a two-three syllable word into its syllabic parts") and RL1PWS-I-5 ("sound out words accurately"), and asks for explicit, systematic instruction and the gradual release of responsibility (the teacher says it, then together, then the child alone). The four steps follow that order. [CURRICULUM; wording read by the team] Young children split syllables more easily than single sounds (Liberman et al., 1974). [RESEARCH: RECALLED]
  • The team's choice. It starts with closed syllables (sunset: sun-set), and uses no open syllables like "baby" at first, because open syllables change in English. No study was found that proves closed syllables must come first. It is common practice in structured literacy programmes. Say that. [TEAM CHOICE]
  • Why only the last step is scored. The parts (bas, ket) are not words, and a speech recognizer is unreliable on isolated non-words.
  • How to say it. "It comes from DepEd's own Grade 1 guide. The closed-syllable order is our choice and we say so."

21Points, streak, weekly goal and badges

  • Points. About half the accuracy percent per real reading (80 percent gives 40). Practice tries and games give none. [TEAM CHOICE]
  • Streak. Days in a row with a real reading, counted in Philippine time. It is worked out from the readings, never stored as a counter, so it cannot be wrong.
  • Weekly goal. 5 real readings a week (one setting).
  • Badges. 100, in ten groups (reading, streak, accuracy, speed, level, goal, points, shelf, games, special). Earned from real history, so a child who already deserves one gets it. [TEAM CHOICE]
  • Why. Small rewards help young children keep reading. The manuscript cites self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci, 2020) and a gamification review (Wang, Harun and Yuan, 2024); check those pages before quoting a finding.
  • What we avoid. No leaderboard that ranks children against each other, no timers that punish, no spending.

23Privacy, consent and safety

  • The law. The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). A child's information is sensitive, and a child cannot consent, so the parent creates the profile and gives two ticks. Everyone has the right to have their data removed (Section 16(e), found by search; read it before quoting).
  • What we did. Parent consent with the policy version recorded; a "last 5 characters" code check that does not reveal who a child is; recordings deleted after scoring (kept only for a few seconds as a temporary file); the activity writer never receives a name; fonts and scripts are hosted by us (no outside tracking); a delete button for a child or an account; an Admin log without personal names.
  • What is still open (say it honestly). Check whether the National Privacy Commission requires registration; a short Privacy Impact Assessment; a breach plan; written agreements with the hosting services; the teammate's reading service has no password yet; no real-child testing yet. The risk register is in docs/legal/LEGAL-RISK-REGISTER.md.
  • How to say it. "We built privacy in: the parent consents, we keep little, we delete recordings, and we show what each outside service receives. We know what remains open and we have a list."

24Why written rules and not a trained model

  • The reasons.
    1. There is no data. Training needs a collection of children's recordings; none exists yet, and collecting them needs consent and an ethics process.
    2. Explainability. A teacher, a parent and an adviser can read a rule and question it. A model gives an answer without a reason.
    3. Safety. A written rule cannot surprise us with an unexpected decision for a child.
    4. It is a better first step. Every number is in one settings file, so once children use the system the team can measure and tune them.
  • What is pretrained and not ours. The speech recognizer (Vosk) and the language model (Gemini). Everything else is rules and formulas.
  • How to say it. "Our contribution is a framework where every adaptive decision has a written rule and a reason, which is the right foundation before any model is trained. We did not train a model, and we say so."

2Revenue streams, one by one

Stream 1: School subscription (the main stream)

  • What it is. A school (or a school division, a local government unit, or a private school) pays a flat fee per month or per school year for its teachers, classes and learners to use the system.
  • Why a flat fee per school. One decision-maker, one invoice, no payment from poor families, and the cost to us hardly changes with the number of learners at the size of a school. [ASSUMPTION]
  • Why not charge families. Many families cannot pay, the learners are children, and charging parents for a learning tool in a public school raises fairness problems.
  • What a fee could look like (examples to calculate with, not a price list): | Option | Example | When it fits | |---|---|---| | Flat per school, per month | PHP 1,500 | A small school, simplest to sell | | Per class, per month | PHP 400 per class | A school that starts with two or three classes | | Per learner, per year | PHP 150 | A division or a private school that wants to pay by head count | Pick one to present. The numbers are [ASSUMPTION]; no school has been asked.
  • Who can actually pay. [TO VALIDATE] Private schools can. Public schools run on limited budgets: a school's Maintenance and Other Operating Expenses (MOOE) and a local government's Special Education Fund (SEF) are the usual places a public school or its division could fund a learning tool. Do not claim a public school can legally buy a software subscription from these funds until you have asked a school head or a DepEd division officer and received it in writing. Other possible payers: a partner company's corporate social responsibility budget, an education NGO, or a donor-funded pilot.

Stream 2: Sale of shared activities (the Open Repository)

  • What it is. An approved teacher lists an activity to the repository as free or paid. A parent unlocks it for a child. The platform could keep a commission and the teacher keeps the rest. Sharing an approved activity also earns a teacher 2 extra writing credits.
  • What exists today. The listing, the unlock, the rating and the "best match for your child first" order all work. The payment is simulated: no real money moves, no payment provider is connected, and no commission is taken.
  • Why it is a supporting stream and not the main one. A paid activity costs a parent a few pesos; even a lively repository brings little (section 4 shows the arithmetic). Its bigger job is to reward teachers for sharing and to build a library, which makes the school subscription worth paying for.
  • What must be built before real money moves [TO VALIDATE]: a payment provider (for example a local e-wallet or card gateway), the gateway's fees, the rules for a refund (the Refund page says what we do today), tax and business registration, and the Terms for sellers. Do not promise a date.

What we do not do

  • No advertisements. The Privacy Policy and the Terms say so. Removing the ads stream is a deliberate trade: less revenue, more trust.
  • No selling of data. Never. Say it plainly if asked.
  • No SMS. It was in version 29; it is not in the system and the update removes it.

Part C. How the system works

C1Walk me through what happens when a parent signs up.

The parent fills the form and ticks the consent box. The server checks the email format and that the domain can receive mail, and suggests a fix for a common typo ("Did you mean gmail.com?"). It saves the account with a hashed password, writes a consent record with the policy version, and sends a confirmation email through the Gmail API. The parent cannot sign in until they open the link. (Details: file 01, scene 1, and file 02.)

C2Where is the password stored?

Only as a hash, never as the password. The sign in is limited to 5 tries and then waits.

C3How does a child sign in without an email?

With the Learner Code (like TB26-48293, with a check digit) and a 4 digit PIN, or by scanning the QR card. The QR holds only the code, never the PIN, so scanning does not sign anyone in. After 5 wrong PINs the child is locked for a short time and the guardians are told once.

C4What if a child forgets the PIN?

Only a linked guardian can make a new one, after typing their own password again. The old PIN stops at once, the child's phone sign ins end, and the other guardians are told. There is a limit of 3 changes a day.

C5How does a teacher join a child to a class?

Typing the last 5 characters of the child's code or scanning the QR. The window checks before Add is pressed: wrong grade, a reading level far above the class, or already in a class all turn Add off with a reason. The child's parents are told.

C6What happens when a child records a reading?

The phone or browser records; the screen checks the internet and wakes the reading service; the recording is uploaded; a background worker sends it to the speech service; the words are lined up against the activity; fairness rules apply; the result is saved; points, badges, notifications and the recommender update; and the child's page shows the result. The recording is deleted when the scoring ends. (File 02, flow D.)

C7Why is scoring in the background?

Scoring takes 5 to 45 seconds. Holding a web request that long used up the few web workers, so the host's health check failed and everyone saw a 502. Now the upload is accepted at once, a worker scores it, and the child's page asks every 2 seconds "is it done?".

C8What was the 502 problem?

The server was running one worker instead of four (a missing flag), so one slow recording blocked everything, including the host's health check, and Render stopped sending traffic. We fixed the flag and moved scoring to the background. We tested with a slow fake service: the health check's slowest answer went from 24 seconds to 1.1.

C9How do the website and the phone app share logic?

The rules live in server services; the website pages and the phone API both call the same services, so they cannot disagree. The phone app only draws what the server says.

C10What is different between the website and the app?

The app is for learner, teacher and parent; the Administrator is website only. The app installs on a phone and signs in with a token; the website uses a session cookie. Teachers drag cards on the website and use buttons on the phone. Scoring in the background is used by the website; the phone waits in the request behind a two-slot guard.

C11What happens if the internet drops mid-reading?

The app checks first; if it cannot reach TaraBasa it shows "Send Again", which keeps the recording. A quick failure is retried automatically up to two times. A drop in the middle of an upload is the one case we cannot recover.

C12Where is the data kept?

In a TiDB Cloud database (MySQL compatible). Recordings are not kept: they wait as a temporary file for seconds and are deleted when scoring ends. Child photos are kept in the database. Sessions are in the database.

C13How are emails sent?

With the Gmail API over HTTPS (Render's free tier blocks the usual mail ports). If the Gmail token expires, nobody can confirm an email; the Admin's System health page shows it and the Admin can confirm an address by hand with a reason that is logged.

C14What does the Administrator do?

Approves teachers (after checking with the school), approves grade requests, deactivates or deletes accounts, confirms an email by hand, and watches System health. Every action is in an Activity log with a reason.

C15Why must the Admin approve teachers?

A teacher can reach children's records. The Admin checks the school and employee ID first. A teacher can sign in before approval but cannot touch real students.

Part D. The AI and the rules

D1Is your AI trained?

No. Say it before they ask. The system uses pretrained services (Vosk for speech, Gemini for drafting activities) and rules and formulas we wrote. Training needs a collection of children's recordings, and none exists yet.

D2Then why do you call it AI?

The speech recognition and the activity writer are AI services, and the system adapts using rules. We call the adaptive part rule-based and explainable. The manuscript uses "AI" for the services and the assistance, not for a trained model of our own.

D3Why not train your own model?

No data, no consent process yet for collecting children's voices, and a model would be harder to explain to a teacher. Our settings are in one file so a future team can replace a rule with a trained model after a pilot supplies data.

D4How does the system decide the child's level?

A first check of up to three texts places the child; after that, three strong readings (90 percent or more, from two different activities) move the child up one step, and two weak readings (under 70) in a row move the child down one. Phil-IRI words are shown to adults. (File 03, sections 6 and 7.)

D5Why 90 and 70?

90 matches the Phil-IRI boundary between instructional and independent. 70 is our alert line, lower than 90 so teachers are not flooded. Both are team settings.

D6Why three readings and not one?

One reading of a short text shows that the child read that text, not that they mastered a step. Reading programmes use repeated evidence. Before this rule, one good reading jumped a child a whole level, which was a bug.

D7What is "Not sure"?

When the recognizer heard a different word but only faintly (confidence under 0.5), the word is left out of the score, the word bank and alerts. At most 25 percent of words can be "not sure", so it cannot hide a reading that is mostly wrong.

D8How do you handle the recognizer being weak on children?

Fairness rules: same-sounding words accepted, spelling variants, numbers, missing apostrophes, compound words heard as two; letter names repaired; faintly heard words left out. Near misses (ripe/right) stay mistakes. Everything shows the teacher the evidence.

D9How do you know the letter repair does not give free marks?

We measured it on 70 live recordings with two computer voices: the rows right went from 14 of 18 to 18 of 18, no score went down, and a row said in the wrong order scored exactly what the service gave. A first, more generous version gave 16.67 to a wrong order, so we rewrote it.

D10How does the AI write an activity? What does it know about the children?

It receives a grade, a skill, a type, a topic and a short note. It knows nothing about any child. If the teacher picks "For a class", our server builds a note from facts at least two children share (the step, the kind of mistake, missed words, liked topics) and the teacher reads it before pressing Write.

D11Could the AI write something wrong or unsafe?

Yes, that is why a teacher must approve each draft. Only approved activities can be given to children. A teacher is told not to type a child's name in the notes, because the text goes to a Google AI model.

D12What is the "Did it work?" card?

It compares the same children's readings of activities written for their class with general activities. It shows a number only when at least 3 children read both, and calls a difference under 3 points "about the same". It is a signal, not proof, because a text can be easier or more interesting.

D13What is the recommender?

A small service with formulas: an attempt score from accuracy, speed and prosody (weights depend on the skill), a slow moving average (0.7 old, 0.3 new), and difficulty bands (easy up to 59, medium up to 79, hard above). We confirmed a live result equals the formula exactly (83.75 then 93.3).

D14What is a "mistake pattern"?

What a child's missed words have in common: small words skipped, word endings, vowel sounds, first or last sounds, blends, look-alike letters, longer words in pieces, and so on. Reported only with at least 3 readings and 6 misses, and if the pattern is 25 percent or more of the misses.

D15How does a suggestion get chosen?

Rules: the teacher's own approved activities, the right grade, not too long for anyone, closest to a comfortable length, matching liked topics and the child's pattern. Rule-based scoring with reasons, not a learned model.

D16Can the system make a wrong decision about a child?

Yes. That is why it moves slowly, why alerts show their evidence, why nothing is given to a child without a teacher or a parent action (apart from the child's own picker, which shows only what was assigned or unlocked), and why a teacher can overrule levels.

D17Can a child cheat?

A child can have help reading. The system cannot tell. Points and badges are small, free practice and games do not change the level, and the level moves only after repeated evidence. A teacher can see the pattern.

D18What does "prosody" mean here?

The expression and rhythm of reading. The reading service gives a heuristic score. It is used only in the fluency formula in the recommender and is never shown to a child.

Part E. Curriculum and Phil-IRI

E1What is the Phil-IRI?

The Philippine Informal Reading Inventory, DepEd's tool to find a learner's reading level: the teacher listens to the learner read graded passages aloud, counts errors, and asks comprehension questions. Policy: DepEd Order No. 14, s. 2018 [CONFIRM the title on deped.gov.ph].

E2What are Independent, Instructional and Frustration?

For word reading in one passage: Independent 97 percent or more (reads alone comfortably), Instructional 90 to 96 (learns with a teacher's guidance), Frustration under 90 (too hard even with help). A Non-reader cannot yet recognize and sound out letters and simple words.

E3Where does "frustration level" come from?

From the informal reading inventory tradition; an older Phil-IRI document says it was adapted from Johnson, Kress and Pikulski (1987) [unverified; confirm in the document before naming them].

E4Do you show children those words?

No. Children see four friendly steps. Parents see the same four steps, with a plain sentence about what that step means. Only teachers see the Phil-IRI words, with the evidence.

E5How did you connect a reading step to a Phil-IRI word?

Our own mapping: Letters is Non-reader; Words and Short sentences are Frustration; Sentences and Short stories are Instructional; Stories and Longer stories are Independent. It is a team choice, said openly.

E6Why are the four steps named Letter Explorer, Word Builder, Sentence Reader and Story Reader?

Because they follow the order MATATAG teaches reading (letters, words, sentences, stories), and the names tell a child what they do, not how they score.

E7What is MATATAG?

The revised basic education curriculum (DepEd Order No. 10, s. 2024). It is written by grade, with codes such as RL1PWS-I-5 ("sound out words accurately"). We attach codes to the first check texts, mistake patterns and activities [CONFIRM the wording against the guides].

E8What does "Hamon: Bawat Bata Bumabasa" mean?

A DepEd reading programme (Memorandum No. 173, s. 2019): "Challenge: Every Child a Reader". It names six elements of reading: oral language, phonological awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, comprehension.

E9Your reading path is not tied to grade. Isn't that against the curriculum?

The class, the activity's grade and the curriculum code follow the grade. The child's reading step decides what the child reads now. A Grade 3 child who cannot read yet should start on letters; the curriculum itself is built to teach those foundations. The grade says where a child is enrolled, not what they can read.

E10Why one grade per class? DepEd has multigrade classes.

DepEd defines a multigrade class as two or more grades under one teacher [CONFIRM the orders, DO 96 s. 1997 and DO 81 s. 2009]. We deliberately did not model it: a teacher with several grades opens one class per grade. The curriculum is by grade and the school decides who teaches which grade, so the Admin confirms a teacher's added grade with the school.

E11Where do Easy, Medium and Hard come from?

They are the app's levels, based on word familiarity and text length, not DepEd scores. The curriculum gives no word counts, so we say so and let the teacher place every activity.

E12What are the length limits based on?

The activity writer's own length bands and the first check's texts. They are team figures; the curriculum gives none.

Part F. UI and UX

F1Why does the child never see a score?

Young children understand a road and a next step better than a percentage, and a low score on a first try should not make a six-year-old feel bad. The result star is always happy. The teacher and parent see the numbers.

F2Why is the text so big, and why five sizes?

Emergent readers need large print, and some need larger. The default is set by grade (Grade 1 largest). The font Lexend was designed for reading comfort; the evidence for it is mixed, so we do not claim it improves reading.

F3Why tap and not drag for children?

A study of 90 children aged 2 to 8 found that 83 percent of the 7 to 8 year olds could tap, 40 percent swipe and 30 percent drag and drop (Yadav et al., 2020). It is a small sample, so it is a reason, not proof.

F4Why does Tara speak every instruction and also show it in text and a glowing button?

A child who cannot read yet can still follow the voice; the text and the glow support children who can read; written prompts alone are not reliable for children of 8 and under.

F5Why do you use windows (dialogs) in the teacher screens?

Teachers asked for it: every action in a window and every screen fitting one laptop view with no scrolling. We measured each screen at a laptop size.

F6Why does a parent's screen show "Word Builder" and not "Frustration" or "Developing"?

A parent sees the same four step names as their child, so they can talk about it together, and a plain sentence says what it means and leads to what to do (the home tip). "Frustration level" sounds harsh to a parent, and the system's own stored level (Beginning, Developing, Proficient) is internal and is shown to no one.

F7How is the design accessible?

Large tap targets (48 points on the phone, 44 pixels on the web), text-size control, contrast, reduced motion respected, a spoken voice, icons not emoji. We reviewed every screen at 360, 390, 430 and 768 pixel widths and at text 20 percent larger. We did not test with children who have disabilities.

F8Why a QR card?

Children have no email; a card they can scan is a fast, low-typing way to enter the code. It holds only the code and never the PIN.

Part G. Privacy, ethics and security

G1What law applies?

The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). A child's data is sensitive and a child cannot consent, so the parent creates the profile and gives two ticks. Everyone can ask for their data to be removed (Section 16(e)) [CONFIRM by reading the Act].

G2What did you do for privacy?

Parent consent with the policy version stored; recordings deleted after scoring; fonts and scripts hosted by us (no outside trackers); the activity writer never gets a name; a delete button for a child or an account; five public legal pages; an Admin log that keeps a reason but not the person's name after deletion.

G3What outside services get data, and what do they get?

Render (hosting) and TiDB (database): the app's data. Google: the Gmail API gets the email text; Gemini gets only a grade, a skill, a topic and a class summary note. The speech service (hosted by our teammate) gets the recording and deletes it after scoring. No analytics, no ads, no trackers. The visitor's browser makes one bare wake-up request to the helper services.

G4Is the speech service secure?

It has no password yet, which is a known risk we list. It would need a key before wide use.

G5What if someone guesses a learner's code?

A code has a check digit and the PIN is needed to sign in; five wrong PINs lock the child. A teacher's lookup by last five characters is limited (20 refusals an hour) and a "yes" never names the child.

G6What did the security audit find?

For example, the seeded Admin password was in a public repository (now set from a secret), a reflected XSS on an internal page (fixed), and lists in the address caused crashes (fixed). We upgraded Laravel after a security notice and added security headers and friendly error pages. Each has a test.

G7Do you keep the child's photo?

Only if the parent adds one with a separate optional consent tick. It is stored in the database (so it no longer disappears when the server restarts) and only the child, a linked guardian and the child's class teacher can see it.

G8Is the system registered with the National Privacy Commission?

Open item. We list it in the risk register as something to check (whether the registration rules apply), together with a Privacy Impact Assessment, a breach plan and written agreements with the hosting services.

G9Can a parent delete everything?

Yes. A guardian removes a child; with another guardian linked it only unlinks the one who asked. A person can delete their own account with their password. The Administrator can do it for someone who cannot sign in.

K1Your adviser said AI. Where is the AI?

The recommender (T, service version 1.0.0)

Foundational: 0.80 accuracy + 0.20 speed. Fluency: 0.40 accuracy + 0.35 speed + 0.25 prosody. Comprehension: quiz score. New score = 0.70 old + 0.30 attempt. Easy up to 59, medium up to 79, hard above. Confidence starts at 0.60, plus 0.08 per attempt, up to 0.95. Worked example: 87.5 x 0.40 + 100 x 0.35 + 55 x 0.25 = 83.75; 97.4 x 0.70 + 83.75 x 0.30 = 93.3.
